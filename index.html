EPRO506 · Assessment 3

Working with Children, Families, and Communities in Contemporary Society

A Digital Portfolio Exploring Family and Community Contexts in Early Childhood. This portfolio is a professional resource for early childhood educators, exploring how five family and community contexts — economic, social, cultural & diversity, health & wellbeing, and crisis & emergency — shape children's development, and how services can respond with evidence-based, relationship-centred practice.

→How to use this portfolio

Use the pathway on the left to move between contexts. Each context follows the same structure: understanding the issue, its impact on children and families, the Australian policy response, five practice strategies, five partner organisations, and resources for educators and children.

Economic Contexts

Families Experiencing Poverty and Housing Stress

Examining how inadequate income and housing instability affect young children and how early childhood services can respond.

Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

01Understanding the Context

This section examines poor and stressed families. In Australia, economic disadvantage is about the capacity of a family to meet basic needs, be part of society, and have access to services. Housing stress, defined as spending more than 30% of household income on rent, leads to instability and to regular move-in and move-outs.

It is important for economic contexts to be understood in ECE because financial difficulties have direct effects on the home environment. The microsystem (direct family) is strongly connected to the exosystem (parental work and government housing policies) in Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory (Bronfenbrenner, 2021). Parents' ability to provide stable routines is reduced by financial stress. Recent studies reveal that the increase in inflation and a serious shortage of rental housing has expanded the family group of those at hardship, and ECE services now serve middle-income families who have been thrust into the hardship category (Healy et al., 2025).

02Impact on Children and Families

Economic hardship has a strong impact on the development and wellbeing of children. Parental mental health issues may be caused by financial stress, which may impact parent-child interactions (Nasr et al., 2025). Children may suffer from food insecurity which can affect their ability to focus and development (Healy et al., 2025). Educators may observe that children are coming to ECE services without enough clothes on, without enough nutritious food, or tired. High mobility by virtue of housing stress, children move in and out of ECE services, this impacts on learning continuity, sense of belonging, and peer relationships (Thorpe et al., 2020). Thus, teachers need to make sure that they provide a consistent, predictable setting to balance home instability.

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

The Child Care Subsidy (CCS) is an income-tested payment reducing ECE costs for low and middle-income families. Additionally, the Early Years Learning Framework (EYLF) requires educators to practice Equity and Inclusion. These policies obligate services to accommodate families facing hardship. For example, under the CCS, services can waive the "Activity Test" for families experiencing temporary financial hardship, ensuring children maintain access to 36 hours of subsidised care per fortnight.

04Strategies for Practice

  • Establish a Clothing Bank: Educators discreetly collect donated second-hand uniforms and weather-appropriate clothing, offering items confidentially to ensure the child is comfortable and included, building self-esteem.
  • Key Educator Approach: Assigning a primary educator to a child experiencing housing instability ensures they have a consistent, secure base, tracking their emotional wellbeing to build resilience.
  • Service-Supplied Meals: Where food insecurity is noticed, educators can provide universal morning tea. This removes the stigma of bringing a "poor" lunchbox and ensures all children have the nutrition needed for cognitive engagement.
  • Flexible Drop-offs: Parents who are feeling financial insecurity and whose transportation may be unreliable may be acknowledged and flexible, non-judgmental morning routines may be adopted to help minimize parent anxiety and improve parent/teacher relationships.
  • Open-Ended Materials: Teachers show children how to play with recycled materials. This is an example of the high quality learning that does not need expensive toys and encourages parents to do the same at home.

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • The Smith Family — A national organization that offers educational assistance. ECE services work together by referring families to the Families' 'Learning for Life' programme, which funds education.
  • St Vincent de Paul Society (Vinnies) — Responds to emergency needs and supplies. ECE services can establish a partnership with local branches to expedite services for families experiencing crisis.
  • Local Council Services — Local governments offer financial counselling and rate/rent relief. Educators provide families with direct contact details or warm referrals.
  • Centrelink / Services Australia — Offers the Temporary Financial Hardship payment. ECE services assist parents by providing necessary paperwork to support their claim.
  • Financial Counselling Australia (FCA) — Offers free, independent financial counselling. An ECE service could host a counsellor for confidential, on-site appointments for stressed parents.

Social Contexts

High-Conflict Divorce/Separation and Out-of-Home Care

Exploring how family breakdown, custody disputes, and out-of-home placements affect a young child's sense of safety and belonging.

Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

01Understanding the Context

In this section, families from a high-conflict divorce/separation and children in Out-of-Home Care (OOHC) are explored. High-conflict divorce is a conflict characterized by constant fighting from both parents and frequent changes in living because of the conflict. Children in OOHC are those who have been taken from their biological parents because of concerns about their safety and placed with another family member or foster family (McNamara et al., 2019).

It is important to recognise these contexts in ECE as they are a factor that can impact on the primary attachment relationships. According to Bowlby's Attachment Theory (Bowlby et al., 1956), a secure primary attachment is the basis for emotional regulation. A secure base is compromised by fracturing of family structures. Current research identifies that the nature of Australian society also has evolved, with an increase in blended and kinship care families, meaning that ECE services need to be highly flexible and accept that the concept of "family" is flexible.

02Impact on Children and Families

The impact of high conflict divorce and OOHC placements is extremely detrimental to children's wellbeing (D'Onofrio & Emery, 2019). They often feel confused, guilty, or grieving, and may regress in their behaviour (wetting the bed, for instance), have trouble paying attention and be anxious with peers. Aggressive outbursts or withdrawal may become evident on transition days between homes for ECE services. Services are required to deal with complicated legal issues and incorporate family court directions in relation to authorised pickups (Knight & Rossi, 2018). The ECE environment can become an important, stable, predictable setting and educators need to scaffold emotionally.

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

Post separation, parental responsibilities are regulated by the Family Law Act 1975, which emphasises what is in the child's best interests (Mcintosh & Booth, 2021). The National Framework for Protecting Australia's Children 2021-2031 is the national child protection strategy for OOHC. These policies have an impact on ECE practice as they set boundaries for practice. The Family Law Act sets requirements for ECE services to ensure that children are not placed in unsafe exchanges of custody. The National Framework reiterates the educator's obligation to be a mandatory reporter of abuse or neglect.

04Strategies for Practice

  • Strict Authorisation Protocols: School staff adhere to authorized Pickup Lists, pursuant to court orders. This includes having physical copies stored at the front desk so that staff do not give a child to a noncustodial parent, avoiding the child from getting caught up in an upsetting situation.
  • Emotion Coaching: Teachers use the "Zones of Regulation" to teach emotional language. A child's response to an out of control behaviour after a foster visit is validated: "I see you're in the red zone, it's difficult when your routine changes, it's ok, you are feeling emotions."
  • Predictable Daily Rituals: Educators make individualized visual schedules to balance instability. When people arrive, reviewing this establishes a sense of safety and predictability.
  • "Safe Space" Transitions: A quiet corner, rich with sensory items, is created for children distressed as a result of a change in custody. The educator takes the child to this place to relax, then brings them back to the group.
  • Inclusive Language: Teachers avoid overgeneralization of terms, such as "grown-ups" instead of "mum and dad" and respect that various family structures are valid, but do not make a child the focus of the question.

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • Family Relationship Centres (FRCs) — State-funded centres that assist separating parents in negotiating parenting plans.
  • DCJ / Child Protection Caseworkers — Oversee children in OOHC. Educators collaborate by sharing observational reports on the child's development and behaviour.
  • CREATE Foundation — A national organisation advocating for children in OOHC. ECE services access their resources to understand foster children's needs.
  • Kinship Care Support Services — Supports relatives raising children. ECE services collaborate by offering flexible drop-off times or connecting carers with respite care.
  • Play Therapists — Professionals using play to help children process trauma. ECE educators collaborate to align the child's emotional goals across both environments.

Cultural & Diversity Contexts

First Nations Families and Refugee Families

Understanding how cultural identity, colonisation, and forced displacement shape children's experiences in early childhood settings.

Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

01Understanding the Context

In this section, First Nations families and refugee families are discussed. First Nations families experience the continuing effects of colonization and continue to live with strong cultural traditions. Refugee families are defined as people who are forced to leave their home country because of persecution or violence, and then come to Australia.

It is important that a child's understanding of these contexts is evident in ECE as culture is the basis of a child's identity and learning. According to Sociocultural Theory of Vygotsky, cognitive development is strongly related to social and cultural contexts (Vygotsky, 2019). These families bring a vast amount of cultural capital with them and they are not being seen and understood in a deficit way by educators. Australian society is evolving with a growing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander population and ongoing humanitarian migration movements and ECE services need to proactively change to ensure they are truly inclusive, culturally safe and supportive environments.

02Impact on Children and Families

Failure of ECE environments to value and accept families' culture can create loss of identity, low self-esteem and disaffection in children (Lamb, 2020). Children in care may be exposed to systemic racism, refugee children to trauma and language barriers, and these factors can have a negative effect on their emotional health, peer relationships and play engagement. In the case of ECE services, educators need to be aware that a child's silence might be a culturally respectful behaviour, and not a developmental delay (Lamb, 2020). Services need to critically examine and understand their own environments and ensure that they do not reinforce biases and tokenise cultures.

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

Culturally competent practice and recognition of the history of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islanders is required by the Early Years Learning Framework (EYLF) (DoE, 2022). In addition, the National Anti-Racism Framework offers a national approach to systemic racism. These policies shift culture from a voluntary to a mandatory professional aspect. The EYLF makes the requirement to embed First Nations perspectives into practice every day, including by requiring the use of local Indigenous languages in routines, and providing access to translated materials for refugee families.

04Strategies for Practice

  • Embed Local First Nations Languages: Teachers work with local Elders to learn and practice greetings and counting in traditional language. It is clearly reflecting the importance of First Nations culture, and instills pride in Indigenous children and respect for Country.
  • "Funds of Knowledge" Approach: Parents are called up to share family skills, such as weaving, cooking, etc. with the children in this service, a recognition of the family's cultural capital and an increase in child confidence.
  • Trauma-Informed Transitions: Educators provide low sensory, predictable transition areas for refugee children who are going through trauma. The use of visual schedules and providing a quiet "cave" area when feeling overwhelmed.
  • Critically Audit Learning Materials: Educators review books to ensure authentic, non-stereotypical representations. They remove stereotypical materials and replace them with books showing resettled families in modern homes, countering biases.
  • Bilingual Communication: Educators use translation apps or visual boards to speak with refugee families in their home language, ensuring parents understand educational goals and fostering respectful partnerships.

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • Local Aboriginal Land Council / Elders — Cultural authorities who guide the service. ECE services collaborate by inviting Elders to conduct Welcome to Country and advise on local histories.
  • STARTTS — A specialised service for trauma survivors. ECE services collaborate by attending training to support traumatised children and referring families for counselling.
  • SNAICC — The national peak body for First Nations children. ECE services use their resources to align practices with Aboriginal child-rearing principles.
  • Local Migrant Resource Centres (MRCs) — Provide settlement support and English classes. ECE services collaborate by co-hosting events or referring newly arrived families.
  • Bicultural Support Workers — Bilingual professionals who support specific families. Services collaborate to facilitate enrolment interviews and help children settle in their mother tongue.

Health & Wellbeing Contexts

Parental Mental Illness, Grief and Trauma

Exploring how parental mental health challenges, childhood grief, and traumatic events affect development and how educators can provide safe, supportive environments.

Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

01Understanding the Context

This section looks at families with a parent who has severe mental illness and children who are experiencing grief and trauma. Mental illness can affect a parent's ability to be consistent and responsive. Grief and trauma are used to describe a child's emotional reactions to loss or events that are so upsetting they are beyond the child's coping mechanisms.

In ECE it is important to understand these contexts as health and wellbeing are inextricably linked with cognitive development. In Bronfenbrenner's Bioecological Model, the parent's mental illness affects "proximal processes" within the microsystem and negatively impacts outcomes. Trauma Theory (Perry, 2006) highlights the developmental changes in the brain due to chronic stress, impacts the amygdala and prefrontal cortex. Recent Australian studies suggest that parents experienced psychological distress in the aftermath of COVID-19 which is likely to increase in the current times, and educators will play a significant role in helping children to cope with psychological distress.

02Impact on Children and Families

Mental illness and trauma can have a significant effect on the development of a child when the parents have been affected. "Parentification" — when children assume responsibility for caregiving — can contribute to anxiety and missing milestones (Kamis, 2020). Emotional dysregulation can result from trauma, either in the form of hyperarousal (aggression) or dissociation (withdrawal). They are unable to concentrate, play and develop close friendships. In the case of ECE services, the educators might observe emotional outbursts, which vary from time to time, regressive behaviours such as a loss of language, or a deep withdrawal. Services need to be a secure base — a safe, low stress environment to counterbalance the chaos or sadness that is often felt at home (Bowlby, 1969).

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

Be You (Beyond Blue) has developed the National Mental Health and Wellbeing Framework for ECE settings to support the development of mental health. One in five adults in Australia now has a mental illness, with the impact of mental illness on parents being reflected in these statistics. These policies are based on the concept of pedagogy and incorporate a trauma-informed perspective. Be You provides services with the resources to develop mental health literacy, moving away from a "managing behaviour" approach and focus on understanding the trauma drivers. This means teachers need to see challenging behaviours as dysregulated stress responses or need to co-regulate these responses.

04Strategies for Practice

  • Predictable Routines and Visual Schedules: Visual schedules are used by teachers to balance unpredictability. When arriving, provide the child with a visual timeline to assure them what is coming next and to promote safety and decrease anxiety.
  • Emotion Coaching: The use of "Zones of Regulation" is used as a framework for teaching emotional vocabulary. If a child is in the red zone, educator support emotional regulation and resilience by saying, "I see you are in the red zone, let's take deep breaths together."
  • Co-Regulation in a Sensory Space: Teachers help a child with hyperarousal to settle in a quiet, dimly lit room with weighted blankets. The educator stays with the child and speaks in a calming tone to help the child's nervous system return to baseline.
  • "Check-In" System: Educators utilize an emotions check-in chart (name to the emotions face). This enables teachers to observe a child's emotional level each day and adjust expectations accordingly.
  • Strength-Based Communication: Educators work with a mentally ill parent in a strengths-based approach. When starting a conversation, it's better to begin with positive comments instead of concerns, as this minimizes parental shame and helps to capture parental attention.

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • Emerging Minds — Specialises in child and family mental health. ECE services collaborate by accessing free online training and sharing resources with parents.
  • Beyond Blue (Be You) — Provides mental health support frameworks. Services collaborate by registering their team for Be You, accessing consultants to support practice.
  • Local Child and Family Health Services (CaFHS) — Provide developmental assessments and counselling. ECE educators collaborate by making warm referrals when trauma severely impacts development.
  • Head to Health — A free national service connecting adults with mental health support. ECE services provide this information confidentially to struggling parents.
  • Play Therapists — Professionals supporting children processing severe trauma. ECE services collaborate to align the child's emotional goals and sensory strategies.

Crisis & Emergency Contexts

Natural Disasters and Domestic and Family Violence

Examining how sudden displacement from disasters and the coercion of domestic violence shatter a child's sense of safety, and how services can act as stabilising forces.

Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

01Understanding the Context

This section explores families displaced by natural disasters (such as bushfires or floods) and families experiencing domestic and family violence (DFV). Natural disasters cause sudden displacement and loss of property. DFV is characterised by coercive, controlling behaviour, often requiring families to flee suddenly to seek safety.

A knowledge of these contexts is important in ECE as both are sudden and dramatic challenges to a child's sense of safety. Within Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs theory (Booth, 2024), physiological and safety needs need to be addressed before a child can attend to the needs of belonging or learning. In both cases basic safety is broken down. Modern research indicates that the natural disaster environment is increasing in terms of its frequency in Australia due to the changing climate, adding to family stress. At the same time, DFV is an ongoing problem, and ECE services have a role to play as key pillars of stability following a crisis (Xu et al., 2023).

02Impact on Children and Families

Sudden crises profoundly impact children's wellbeing. The lack of predictability and violence triggers acute stress, trauma and hyperarousal (Eadie et al., 2021). Children may have difficulties sleeping, have a high attachment to or separation anxiety from the parent, and regress in skill development (speech, potty training) (Gildersleeve et al., 2022). They might repeat their play which reinforces the crisis, affecting their peers' play and cognition. If providing ECE services, educators might observe sudden shifts in behaviour, distress during transitions or aggressive outbursts (Meyer et al., 2021). Services need to address sudden withdrawal, work with children who have lost their homes and follow safety measures if a violent parent tries to access the service.

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

Key responses are the National Plan to End Violence against Women and Children 2022–2032 and the Disaster Recovery Funding Arrangements (DRFA). According to AIHW (2023), statistically, 1 in 6 women will be physically or sexually violated by a current or former partner (Department of Social Services, 2022). Such policies include rigorous safety measures. The National Plan requires ECE services to have domestic violence safety plans, which must include strict pick up procedures. The DRFA enables ECE services to serve as community recovery hubs, waiving fees or offering emergency placements to children who have been displaced, until their parents can recover, giving children a safe place to be while parents recover.

04Strategies for Practice

  • Strict Safety Protocols: In the case of DFV contexts, teachers implement court mandates. This includes maintaining a photo of the non-custodial parent at the front desk, staff members promptly locking doors and alerting the police if the doors are unlocked, and physically ensuring that the child is protected.
  • "Safe Base" Transition Space: Educators have a quiet corner for displaced children that has sensory tools available. The educator sits with the child to provide physical co-regulation and eases the child out of hyperarousal into safety.
  • Expressive Play Opportunities: Teachers offer open-ended resources such as sand or blocks. The educator sits beside a child as they relive a flood and has patience to support the child in processing their trauma and building resilience ("The people are safe now").
  • Predictable Daily Rituals: Teachers take time to explicitly discuss the daily schedule to counteract chaos. Use a visual timeline and remind them, "First morning tea, then you go home with grandma."
  • Practical Material Support: Educators, in a discreet manner, present "care packs" of spare clothes/donated toys for families in displacement. This non-judgmental support helps to decrease parental stress and has a positive effect on the emotional state of the child.

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • 1800RESPECT — The national DFV counselling service. ECE services collaborate by displaying their materials in private spaces and referring parents to their 24/7 helpline for safety planning.
  • Local Women's Refuges — Provide emergency accommodation. ECE services collaborate by offering flexible, immediate enrolment for children residing at the refuge.
  • Australian Red Cross — Vital in disaster recovery. ECE services collaborate by hosting Red Cross recovery sessions or distributing emergency financial aid vouchers to displaced families.
  • Local Government Emergency Management Officers — Coordinate disaster response. Services collaborate by registering their facility as a relief centre and attending local disaster meetings.
  • DCJ Caseworkers — Manage the safety of children experiencing severe DFV. Educators collaborate by providing detailed, objective observational reports to assist in risk assessments.

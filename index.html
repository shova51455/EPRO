Working with Children, Families, and Communities in Contemporary Society

A Digital Portfolio Exploring Family and Community Contexts in Early Childhood. This portfolio is a professional resource for early childhood educators, exploring how five family and community contexts — economic, social, cultural & diversity, health & wellbeing, and crisis & emergency — shape children's development, and how services can respond with evidence-based, relationship-centred practice.

→How to use this portfolio

Use the pathway on the left to move between contexts. Each context follows the same structure: understanding the issue, its impact on children and families, the Australian policy response, five practice strategies, five partner organisations, and resources for educators and children.

Economic Contexts

Financial Hardship, Poverty & Housing Stress

Exploring the impact of inadequate income and unstable housing on the early years of childhood and the important role early learning services play in responding to this.

Family Types Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

Family Types in This Context

Single-parent familiesSingle-parent families have the largest proportion of those living below the poverty line, with more than a third of all single-parent families living below this line. Kids in these families are more likely to frequently change homes and less likely to receive monetary perks like extracurricular activities or outings.
Multi-generational householdsAlongside affordable rents, multi-generational households (a traditional unit of family members living together, including grandparents, parents, and children, sometimes forced into this configuration when rents are too high to afford alone) are another way of relieving housing strain. This can help provide additional hands for caring, though for some children, the added closeness of living together too many times can be temporary disruptions to the routine and stability that they need.

01Understanding the Context

Economic context is about the reality of a family's material life: the things that they earn, the places that they live, and the things that a family can afford. The cost-of-living squeeze has been growing for Australian families with young children since 2021, amidst a steady increase in rents in relation to wages and income payments. The burden on sole parents is the highest, with over one third of that group living below the poverty line, and a projection that in Australia, approximately 15.6% of children will fall into poverty by 2025 (Duncan, 2025).

This is extremely important in early years, because there are two theories which explain why that is so. The family investment model suggests that children from poor families have less stimulating input, and the family stress model suggests increased reactive parenting and less predictability when a family is financially stressed (Masarik & Conger, 2017). Both ideas are contained within Bronfenbrenner's bioecological model, whereby disadvantages at the exo level (e.g., job security, housing, policy & welfare receipt) are transferred to the family day-to-day (Zhang & Han, 2020). This is less of a fringe issue and more a significant matter, as not only is one family relying on income support, but other dual-income households which had previously held decent incomes have been impacted due to rising living costs.

02Impact on Children and Families

Environmental factors are most important in shaping brain architecture during the first five years of life (from prenatal to school-age periods). Poor nutrition, substandard housing and health services may stunt as much physical development as mental growth (O’Donnell & Kingsley, 2020), and the Centre for Community Child Health notes that parents in poverty are two to three times more likely to end up suffering from mental ill-health (Centre for Community Child Health, 2020).

In services, the pressures manifest in clear barriers to participation such as gap fees, uniforms, transport costs, and excursion fees, which exclude children who would most benefit from attending (and disrupting an avenue of care young children relied on if they were to be excluded), and housing-related relocations which break care continuity for young children.

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

There are several policy instruments that can help overcome these obstacles. Providing a minimum of three subsidised days of early learning for families per fortnight (in the current Child Care Subsidy settings) helps reduce cost as a barrier to attendance for families, and further reduces that cost for First Nations children (Duncan, 2025). Other funding is also available through the Inclusion Support Program to increase the capacity of services to respond to the needs of children with additional support needs and through a national Early Years Strategy and state-based First 2000 Days plans, which have a more systemic approach across health, education and families support.

Over 180 organisations support this cause, with The End Child Poverty campaign seeking a national Child Poverty Reduction Act with legislated, time-bound targets. Inclusion funding and subsidy arrangements are what a family in need has to access in practice, and gaps in local services are the place where an educator has to make links to local people.

04Strategies for Practice

  1. Fee flexibility and priority access. Adapt Child Care Subsidy enrolment and gap fees based on discretion that has been programmed into the priority of access for Child Care Subsidy; a child should never be deprived because of financial factors.
  2. Discreet, dignity-preserving practical support. Establish normal systems of maintaining extra clothing, food, or hygiene supplies away from where a child might be singled out to search for them.
  3. Strengthening the home learning environment. Provide simple, low-cost ideas for play and learning and literacy, based on the philosophy of parents as first teachers, as seen in the traditions of programs such as HIPPY.
  4. Warm referral pathways. Develop relationships in real life with financial counselling, housing, and family services with a proper referral, not a number stuck on a noticeboard.
  5. Trauma-informed, relationship-based practice. Understand that stress-induced behaviour is not a 'parenting failure', and respond consistently and co-regulating (not excluding parents).

  • Brotherhood of St Laurence — A social justice group that provides early-years services and advocates on child poverty issues as a delivery partner.
  • Goodstart Early Learning — Founded by BSL, Benevolent Society, Mission Australia and Social Ventures Australia, Australia's largest not-for-profit early learning provider and a national champion for fair access.
  • Services Australia — Ensures that the administration of the Child Care Subsidy and Family Tax Benefit is carried out and links families with financial counselling and concessions.
  • Local Communities for Children Facilitating Partners — Place-based partnerships which are established by government investment to coordinate place-based family services in poor place-based areas.

Social Contexts

Separation, Isolation & Out-of-Home Care

The impact of the breakdown of relationships, of being taken out of the family's social network and from the family home on a young child's sense of safety and belonging.

Family Types Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

Family Types in This Context

Separated and divorced families with shared careChildren who are separated or divorced from their parents usually live with both parents and are in two homes. Helpful, low-ebb communication between these parents and service, on a regular basis, helps ease this adjustment.
Kinship and foster care familiesThere may be information the carers have about the child, but that may not be sufficient, as they are placed away from their birth parents – kinship or foster parents. For these children, it is not unusual for them to have to get used to multiple new surroundings: new home, new school, and new community.

01Understanding the Context

Social context involves a family's relationships and supports (whether they are maintained, broken up, or affected by statutory involvement). Not so unusual occurrences – 52.2 per thousand Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children are in out-of-home care, and the number continues to grow, above the National Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Children's Care and Survival Council target of 10 per thousand (Blackstock et al., 2020).

Understanding why consistent, responsive relationships are important in early childhood is explained through attachment theory – disrupted relationships through conflict, separation, or over changes of home can affect the internal working model that the child has about relationships (Bacro & Macario de Medeiros, 2021). The chronosystem is an additional one: the events do not happen at a single moment only, but take place over time; their influence accumulates over the period of time. It's worth noting here that it isn't separation, but conflict as it happens that most accurately determines the child's adjustment.

02Impact on Children and Families

In young children, parental conflict and changing caring routines are associated with disorganised attachment patterns and, even in the absence of conflict, a significant change in caregiving context can result in temporary separation anxiety and/or issues with regulation (Bacro & Macario de Medeiros, 2021). Out-of-home placements often involve a shift from parents to a placement that is disconnected from parents, and children will often bring relational trauma with them (Laurens et al., 2020). There is also an indirect risk with social isolation because parents with few social relationships receive less support from others, and this is related to the level of parenting strain and to the decrease of parent-child interaction.

In services, signals can be practical, such as: unawareness of one's presence, sudden change in who collects a child, behaviour shown ignores a child's true intention; it is more a reflection of an experience. During times when the world around them is in turmoil, they can find one place where things are reasonably stable, and that place is having a relationship with an educator they know.

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

Safe and Supported — the National Framework for Protecting Australia's Children 2021–2031 — sets the national agenda for preventing child abuse and neglect, and includes a First Action Plan specifically for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children. Meanwhile, in NSW, the Children, Youth and Families Amendment (Stability) Act 2026 scrapped the previous strict 12- and 24-month timelines for reunification, and in Victoria, the 2025 budgetary submission allocated $1.2 billion to child protection, with $797 million dedicated to out-of-home care.

This amount of investment, but the overall progress towards addressing Aboriginal over-representation in out-of-home care is not on track, and it is clear that there is a need to place greater decision-making and funding power with Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisations. The times of educators using caseworkers, kinship carers, and foster carers can all be at about the same time, and they need to be prepared for a child's situation to drastically alter, particularly at the point of reunification.

04Strategies for Practice

  1. Predictable routines and a stable key-educator relationship. Provide a secure and consistent sanctuary on the service day—particularly helpful for children who are experiencing a less predictable life at home.
  2. Separate, low-conflict communication channels. Use individual take-home diaries or apps/photographs so that a child is never at the centre of their parents' communication.
  3. Trauma-informed enrolment and transition practices. Do not adopt the first day-in-caretenant approach with children entering care, but rather have a primary caregiving approach and an unhurried, gradual settling-in period.
  4. Deliberate connection-building for isolated families. Identify low-key, informal venues/sessions for parents to think about expanding their social network, such as a drop-in morning tea or playgroup.
  5. Collaborative, privacy-appropriate information sharing. Implement simple procedures with case workers/carers and inform educators what impact the child is having, but do not provide any more detail than is necessary.

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • Barnardos Australia — Provides family support, kinship care and fostering services and helps to ensure that children are not placed in care unless this is safe to do.
  • Relationships Australia — Provides choices and options for families to refer to services for family relationship counselling, mediation and post-separation parenting support.
  • Playgroup Australia — A low-cost, low-barrier method of "social connections" introduction to isolated families with young children.
  • Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisations (e.g., state peak bodies affiliated with SNAICC) — Provide culturally-safe family support and be an active member of reunification and prevention activities in First Nations families.
  • Local Community and Family Support / targeted early intervention providers — A system moving fairly, but also coordinated, by the government of providing case management, counselling and practical support to families, before it reaches crisis.

Cultural & Diversity Contexts

First Nations, Migrant & Refugee Families

The impact of personal cultural identity, language and the settlement journey on the sense of belonging of a young child and what culturally safe practice might mean in a setting in Australia.

Family Types Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

Family Types in This Context

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander familiesMany Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander families are extended families and community-based and are intergenerational. Children's wellbeing and sense of identity are strengthened by their cultural, language and Country connection.
Refugee and migrant familiesRefugee/migrant families may be new arrivals or in the process of becoming settled, and may be adjusting to a new language and new system, while at the same time facing trauma prior to departure, and the separation from family still overseas, as well as very real challenges of living in Australia.

01Understanding the Context

Cultural and diversity contexts include Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander families, as well as migrant and refugee families which may have a different language, culture and worldview from the dominant culture of a service. That is no small measure – nearly one third of Australians speak a language other than English, with a national plan calling for a 95% target for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in the year before school, and a significant variation in progress towards that target by region.

The macrosystem, or the broader culture, norms, laws and customs in which a family operates, accounts for why there might be a disconnect between family culture and service culture that reduces participation. Culturally Responsive Pedagogy works differently - it does not merely pass education over a cultural "hat"; it is simply a matter of understanding and incorporating cultural difference as a resource into the curriculum (Sisson et al., 2020). Settlement, even for refugee-background families, is seldom a simple process, but may involve additional separation from family, and also involve family life in a refugee context of ongoing struggles to create a social and economic life with young children (Lamb, 2020).

02Impact on Children and Families

Racism and discrimination (social or systemic) can compound or add to trauma, reduce trust in Mainstream services, and impact the wellbeing of First Nations children (Sisson et al., 2020). Refugee and migrant families frequently are concurrent at multiple risk points – displacement, settlement, and limited English proficiency and often deal with various stressors during the years children are developing the most – with flow-on effects to parenting.

This is a measurable access gap, as children from migrant and refugee backgrounds are half as likely to access early-intervention services like speech therapy when there is a need. Inadequate enrolment is more likely a lack of confidence, or experience with being turned down for enrolment in the system, than a lack of interest (Lamb, 2020).

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

The National Agreement for Closing the Gap reflects that targeted enrolment of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in the year before school is the focus of Target 3 but would go further than 95% as this has not yet been achieved; the NSW First Steps strategy provides support to ensure this target is addressed. Sector Strengthening Partnerships with Aboriginal human services continue to increase Aboriginal Community Controlled early childhood services – where evidence demonstrates Aboriginal-led services create better outcomes than those designed and delivered by service providers outside Aboriginal Communities.

The Humanitarian Settlement Program provides structured case management and practical assistance to migrant and refugee families during their initial 18 months in Australia, and community-based settlement will assist families to access their local community schools and early years services. These frameworks are directly relevant to practice as they outline what cultural safety training and access to interpreters are available to them, and how they need to develop their local connections to bridge those areas where they are not available.

04Strategies for Practice

  1. Embed First Nations perspectives daily, not just for NAIDOC Week. Incorporate Acknowledgement of Country, utilize local language (where appropriate); strongly advocate for the use of culturally appropriate resources from Aboriginal-owned publishers in both everyday practices and on a regular basis.
  2. Partner with bicultural and bilingual workers. Always utilize professional interpreters when enrolling, orienting, or having important conversations — never use a child or family member as an interpreter.
  3. Assess and access funds of knowledge. Extend families' input into the curriculum in their own language, stories, food, and celebrations, and not just during one “cultural day”.
  4. Build staff cultural safety through ongoing reflective practice. Conduct ongoing examination of assumptions and biases as a team, rather than assume cultural competence is an "event" that can just be delivered at one point in time.
  5. Allow flexible, patient orientation for newly arrived families. Utilise visual supports, give reasonable time, and do not assume understanding when discussing how the Australian early childhood system works.

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • SNAICC – National Voice for our Children — The national peak body for culturally safe early childhood practice and Aboriginal-led service delivery.
  • Local Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisation (e.g., the Victorian Aboriginal Child Care Agency) — Provides culturally based family and early years services and can lead a service on a journey towards cultural safety.
  • AMES Australia / Settlement Services International — Offers settlement case management, English language assistance and connection with the community to refugee and migrant families.
  • Local multicultural resource centre — Provides Interpreting services and multicultural playgroups for newly arrived families to help connect them with informal networks.
  • Specialist torture and trauma support services (e.g., STARTTS in NSW) — provide trauma-informed counselling services for refugee background families to which services can refer.

Health & Wellbeing Contexts

Parental Mental Illness, Substance Use, Grief & Trauma

Awareness of the impact that the health and wellbeing issues within a family can have on a young child's sense of safety and the role services can play in providing a consistent care experience, not just when a crisis occurs.

Family Types Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

Family Types in This Context

Families where a parent lives with a mental illnessMany Australian families with children have a parent suffering mental illness. The parents' ability to care for their child can change as the child becomes more ill, and children within these families may be doing more than is appropriate for their age.
Families affected by parental substance use or complicated griefParents who have a history of substance use or unresolved grief may have inconsistent or substandard parenting that increases the risk for neglect or instability. These families typically require ongoing, non-judgemental support, rather than a quick fix.

01Understanding the Context

Different areas of health and wellbeing include parents' mental illness, substance abuse, trauma, grief, and abuse, and affect a significant proportion of Australian families with young children. Almost half (40%) of Australian adults say they experienced at least one traumatic early experience; over a fifth (22%) felt it before the age of 10; more than a million Australian children have a parent who suffers from mental illness (Crouch et al., 2019). These experiences are most commonly reported to be bereavement, sexual assault and witnessing family violence (Emerging Minds & ANU, 2020).

Studies on Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) reveal that there is a dose-response effect (Emerging Minds & ANU, 2020): the more experiences a child has of Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) and the sooner these A.C.Es happen, the more seriously it can impact the child's health later in life. For the day-to-day explanation, attachment and trauma theory help by stating that someone with mental illness or using drugs or alcohol may lack the ability to be present for their child, and a young child's brain is sensitive to being seen and heard erratically by their caregiver (Letcher et al., 2020). This awareness has led to increased awareness of infant mental health in Australia and the national children's mental health and wellbeing strategy, which was released in 2018.

02Impact on Children and Families

A mental illness among parents increases the risk of family dysfunction and of having to care for the parent at an age inappropriate for them (Letcher et al., 2020). Parental drug and alcohol use also increases the risk of neglect and ongoing substance-use issues, and family violence exposure is linked to poor educational outcomes, less social engagement, and increased service use by adults (Emerging Minds & ANU, 2020).

In services, these situations are more likely to manifest themselves as dysregulated behaviour, poor attendance, or a child that seems ‘over' or ‘underway ' in his or her behaviour. These are coping strategies – not behavioural – and need a trauma-led approach – not a behaviourist approach.

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

Australia's world-first Department of Health mental health and wellbeing strategy for children (birth to age 12 years) has been implemented in 2021 and is supported by investment in Head to Health Kids centres and an increased focus on perinatal mental health supports for children. National guidance now includes mental health screening as a regular part of the early childhood health checks from 2025, and the Be You programme also supports services to implement a wellbeing plan rather than managing individual situations.

These frameworks put the onus on educators to act more proactively than reactively to support wellbeing, and require them to rely on nationally developed, evidence-based wellbeing programs rather than creating a reactionary approach of their own.

04Strategies for Practice

  1. Trauma-informed, predictable environments. Make use of familiar routines, visual schedules and calming transitions to minimize a dysregulated child's expectations of the world to go crazy around without warning.
  2. Co-regulation before correction. Take time to help calm down a child before dealing with behaviour issues so that they are in a space where they can reflect on their behaviour.
  3. Whole-team wellbeing planning. Have a service-wide wellbeing strategy in place, with supporting documentation, and based on the Be You approach rather than giving staff freedom to respond individually.
  4. Sensitive, non-judgemental conversations with parents. Normalise the reporting of mental health issues or addiction problems to family members and ensure that people know that the service is for support, not judgment.
  5. Warm referral to specialist supports. Link families to specialist services, e.g. Emerging Minds/COPMI and PANDA (direct access).

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • Emerging Minds / COPMI — National service and information centre for children who have parents with a mental illness and their families.
  • PANDA (Perinatal Anxiety & Depression Australia) — Lead National Organisation for anyone who is pregnant and/or within six months of giving birth who is feeling anxious and/or depressed.
  • Local Child and Family Health nursing services — A non-stigmatising, universal contact point, well situated to identify and listen to children at an early age and make a gentle referral.
  • Kookaburra Kids — Offers peer support and free camps for kids with a family member who is suffering from a Mental Disorder.
  • Local trauma and family violence counselling services (e.g., the Blue Knot Foundation) — professional services the family is referred to, if any.

Crisis & Emergency Contexts

Disaster, Displacement & Family Violence

The impact of climate change disasters, as well as family violence and family displacement, on the sense of safety of young children, and how services can act before, during and after a crisis.

Family Types Understanding Impact Policy Strategies Partnerships Resources

Family Types in This Context

Families displaced by natural disasterFamilies displaced by bushfire, flood or cyclone almost invariably are forced to seek temporary or permanent new homes and face the loss of their house, their normal way of life, belongings and social networks – all in one.
Families experiencing family violenceOften, family violence survivors in families are led by parents who have been travelling from an unsafe home, or are planning to leave. Support needs to be undertaken with care and based on the principles of safety planning, confidentiality and sensitive information sharing.

01Understanding the Context

Crisis and/or emergency contexts refer to natural disasters exacerbated by climate change as well as family and domestic and sexual violence, and resultant displacement. Children are a disproportionately large part of the casualties of climate disasters, with, on average, more than 1.4 million (one in six) children affected by them each year (McGill et al., 2023), and those children most vulnerable tend to live in remote areas, with low socio-economic status and Indigenous communities. As a consequence, each year 800,000 days of early learning opportunities are lost.

The Harvard Center on the Developing Child's Toxic Stress Model is the more prominent explanation for how crisis could impact development (Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University, 2020). It sets aside normal, temporary activation of stress systems from those that occur repeatedly in time or forever without a supportive adult to buffer them — something that sooner or later can cause disruption to the developing brain architecture. Violence and disasters constitute another form of jolting change to a child's world, from home to service to community, that could be placed in Bronfenbrenner's chronosystem (Zhang & Han, 2020).

02Impact on Children and Families

Following the 2011 Brisbane floods, a study of 2-year-olds showed that cognitive functioning, vocabulary, and imaginative play were impaired in those exposed to the flooding event while in the fetus' womb (McGill et al., 2023). Between 12-14 months after a disaster, post-traumatic stress may range up to 95% for kids, and most will gradually get better without changes to their caring relationships or routines (McGill et al., 2023). Low school attendance and increased adult contact with mental health services are related to exposure to family violence even as other factors are held constant.

The losses of education, mental health and housing disruption due to disasters in Australia have been estimated to be approaching $6.3 billion annually to Australian children and young people and will continue to rise if Australia does not take more action on climate change (McGill et al., 2023). In the context of services, this manifests itself in the direct disruption through closure, evacuation/relocation, all the while an increasing number of children arriving as a function of crisis in other aspects of their family life.

03Social Policy and Australian Responses

The National Plan to End Violence against Women and Children 2022-2032 was enhanced by the inclusion of a dedicated First Nations plan, Our Ways – Strong Ways – Our Voice, in February 2026, in recognition of the disproportionate levels of violence that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women and children face. There is a national child-centred approach to recovery, to support and draw on the Australian Institute for Disaster Resilience's national recovery principles, and other organisations, including Emerging Minds, have introduced disaster-specific elements to national wellbeing frameworks.

The Domestic, Family and Sexual Violence Commission's 2025 Yearly Report to Parliament highlighted that there was a mismatch between policy aims and practice in relation to meeting the needs of children and that prevention starts from a young age. Services are increasingly now required to have a disaster preparedness and recovery plan as well as a family violence response plan as ‘standard operating procedures'.

04Strategies for Practice

  1. Embed age-appropriate disaster preparedness into the curriculum. Red Cross Pillowcase Workshops allow children to feel that they have some control over how they prepare themselves for disaster, instead of a sense of fear.
  2. Protect routine and predictability immediately after a crisis. Invariably, schedules and familiar caring relationships indicate maximum chances for a child recovering.
  3. Use trauma-informed, child-led recovery activities. Assistive activities such as the “Birdie” nest provide the child with the opportunity to process his or her experience by playing.
  4. Build a documented family violence response protocol. Have safe enrolment and collection procedures written and ensure staff are trained to recognise and respond to play cues that indicate disclosure.
  5. Coordinate directly with emergency and family violence services. Develop these partnerships before a crisis happens to ease the way for referral paths when one does.

05Community and Professional Partnerships

  • Emerging Minds — Durations will be shorter on disaster-specific mental health tools and resources for children/educators, including the Community Trauma Toolkit.
  • Australian Red Cross — Provides support at the Emergency Evacuation Centre, Pillowcase Workshops and child-friendly centres.
  • Local family, domestic and sexual violence service — Hands-on specialist safety planning and counselling, or offers a pathway services can signpost families to.
  • Royal Far West — Implements a self-resourcing bushfire/disaster recovery programme for the wellbeing of young children.
  • Be You — A national wellbeing framework that underpins services responding to disaster or community trauma.
